The turning point

The Revolt of 1857

A cartridge, a mutiny, an empire on fire. The uprising that began in the Bengal Army in May 1857 became the greatest challenge to British rule in the nineteenth century — and the event after which India was never the same.

Causes The spark Leaders Aftermath Mutiny or war?
Background

A Company on the edge of its authority

By 1857 the East India Company governed most of the subcontinent — directly in the Presidencies of Bengal, Madras and Bombay, and indirectly through hundreds of princely states. A private trading corporation had become a sovereign power, collecting revenue, administering justice and maintaining an army larger than that of many European states. That army was overwhelmingly Indian: by the 1850s, roughly four out of five soldiers under Company colours were Indian sepoys.

Company expansion had been relentless. Dalhousie's Doctrine of Lapse annexed states without a natural heir; Awadh was annexed in 1856 on grounds of misgovernment, and the pensions of the Mughal court were squeezed. Land revenue was heavy and often inflexible, newly enriched landlords and moneylenders pressed peasants, and British manufactures displaced Indian handicrafts. The reforms of the preceding decades — the abolition of sati in 1829, the Widow Remarriage Act of 1856, missionary activity and English education — bred suspicion among orthodox groups. And in the army, sepoys resented pay, allowances and the ceiling on promotion.

Political grievances

The Doctrine of Lapse and annexations of Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur and Awadh; the end of dynastic pensions; the humiliation of the Mughal emperor; the fear that treaties and titles meant nothing.

Economic grievances

High land revenue, resumption of rent-free lands, peasant indebtedness, the decline of Indian textiles, the drain of wealth to Britain, and sepoys' complaints over pay and allowances.

Military grievances

The new Enfield rifle's greased cartridges, which had to be bitten open; the General Service Enlistment Act requiring overseas service; the small number of British officers and no path to promotion for Indians.

Social and religious fears

Fear of forced conversion and of reforms that seemed to attack caste and religion. The cartridge rumour — beef and pork fat — touched both Hindu and Muslim soldiers at once.

29 March – 20 September 1857

The spark, and the spread

What began as a mutiny in one cantonment became a regional uprising — and then a war with many centres and many motives.

29 Mar

Barrackpore

Mangal Pandey of the 34th Bengal Native Infantry attacks his officers, calling on comrades to rise. He is court-martialled and hanged on 8 April. The regiment is disbanded.

10 May

Meerut

On a Sunday evening, sepoys of the 3rd Light Cavalry and 11th and 20th Infantry break out, free their jailed comrades, and march through the night towards Delhi.

11 May

Delhi

The rebels seize Delhi and proclaim the aged Bahadur Shah Zafar emperor. The name of the Mughal gives the revolt a legitimacy that no single sepoy could.

Jun–Jul

Kanpur and Lucknow

Nana Saheb raises Kanpur; Begum Hazrat Mahal leads Awadh from Lucknow. At Kanpur, besieged British survivors are massacred after a promised safe passage; British reprisals are brutal.

20 Sep

Delhi recaptured

British columns storm Delhi after a siege. Zafar is arrested; two of his sons are shot. The recapture breaks the symbolic centre of the revolt.

1858

Jhansi and Gwalior

Rani Lakshmibai and Tantia Tope defend Jhansi, then seize Gwalior. On 18 June 1858 the Rani is killed in battle; Tantia continues a guerrilla war until his capture and execution in 1859.

Other centres

Kunwar Singh in Bihar, Khan Bahadur Khan in Bareilly, Maulvi Ahmadullah Shah in Awadh, and countless local leaders kept the rebellion alive well after Delhi fell. The revolt was never one army with one plan — which is both its weakness and its breadth.

The leaders

Faces of the uprising

BZ

Bahadur Shah Zafar

1775–1862Mughal emperor

The last Mughal emperor, and the revolt's reluctant figurehead. Tried in 1858, he was exiled to Rangoon, where he died in 1862. The dynasty ended with him.

RL

Rani Lakshmibai

1828–1858Queen of Jhansi

After Jhansi was annexed under the Doctrine of Lapse, she fought to restore her adopted son's claim. She became the most enduring symbol of resistance, dying in battle at Gwalior.

NS

Nana Saheb

1824–c.1859Peshwa claimant

Denied his adoptive father's pension, he led the Kanpur uprising and was proclaimed Peshwa. His fate after 1859 is unknown; he disappeared from the record.

TT

Tantia Tope

1814–1859General

Nana Saheb's general and the revolt's most capable guerrilla commander, he fought across central India with Lakshmibai and held out long after the major centres fell.

HM

Begum Hazrat Mahal

c.1820–1879Regent of Awadh

She led the Awadh resistance after her son was denied the throne, organised government in Lucknow, and refused amnesty. She died in exile in Nepal.

KS

Kunwar Singh

1777–1858Zamindar of Jagdishpur

A veteran zamindar of Bihar in his eighties, he led a formidable local army, captured Arrah, and died of wounds shortly after his final victory.

Aftermath

The Company goes, the Crown arrives

The revolt was suppressed with a ferocity that shocked even contemporaries: villages burned, rebels blown from cannon, and summary executions across the Gangetic plain. Estimates of the dead vary enormously, but the human cost was immense on all sides. For the British, the lesson was that India could not be left to a trading company.

Government of India Act 1858

The Company was abolished. India came under the direct rule of the Crown, exercised through a Secretary of State in London and a Viceroy in India. The Governor-General became the Viceroy; Canning was the first.

The Queen's Proclamation

Read at Allahabad on 1 November 1858, it promised religious neutrality, an end to annexation, respect for treaties with princes, and clemency for rebels not guilty of murder.

The army reorganised

The ratio of British to Indian troops was raised, artillery was kept in British hands, and regiments were mixed by caste and region to prevent another coordinated mutiny.

Divide and rule

The Raj would now cultivate princes, landlords and religious communities as counterweights to nationalism — a strategy that shaped Indian politics for the next ninety years.

Mutiny or First War of Independence?

British accounts long called it the “Sepoy Mutiny” — a military disturbance. Indian nationalists, most famously V. D. Savarkar in 1909, called it the First War of Independence. Most historians today see something between the two: a broad anti-colonial uprising with real political aims, but limited coordination, uneven participation and many local grievances. What is not in doubt is its consequence — the end of Company rule and the beginning of the Raj.
