The long struggle

The Freedom Struggle, 1905–1947

From petitions to mass movements, from the Swadeshi boycotts to Quit India: how a colonised subcontinent organised itself into the largest anti-colonial movement in history.

1885–1919

From petition to mass politics

The Indian National Congress was founded in 1885 as a platform for the Western-educated professional classes. Its early “Moderate” leaders — Dadabhai Naoroji, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta — believed in constitutional agitation: petitions, resolutions, economic critique and gradual reform within the Empire. They won little immediately, but they built an organisation, a press and a public language of rights.

By 1905 a younger “Extremist” wing had grown impatient. When Curzon partitioned Bengal, the Swadeshi movement answered with boycotts of British cloth, the promotion of Indian industry, national schools and the slogan Bande Mataram. Leaders such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Lala Lajpat Rai and Bipin Chandra Pal — the trio known as Lal-Bal-Pal — pushed for swaraj, self-rule, as the goal. The Muslim League was founded in 1906, and the Morley–Minto reforms of 1909 introduced separate electorates for Muslims, deepening communal division in constitutional politics.

The First World War (1914–18) changed everything. Indian soldiers fought for the Empire while prices rose and freedoms were restricted. The Lucknow Pact of 1916 reunited Congress and the League around a shared demand for greater self-government, and Tilak and Annie Besant launched Home Rule Leagues. Then, in 1919, the Rowlatt Act and the Jallianwala Bagh massacre shattered any remaining faith in British benevolence.

Moderates

Naoroji, Gokhale and their colleagues used economic argument and constitutional pressure. Their drain theory and budget critiques laid the intellectual foundation of nationalism.

Extremists

Tilak, Lajpat Rai and Bipin Chandra Pal demanded swaraj and mass mobilisation. Tilak's newspapers and his popular base in Maharashtra made nationalism a public cause.

Swadeshi

The boycott of British goods and the promotion of Indian enterprise turned nationalism into an economic programme and a daily practice for ordinary people.

1915–1942

Gandhi and the mass movement

Gandhi returned from South Africa in 1915 and, over the next three decades, transformed the Congress into a mass organisation. His campaigns are treated in full on the Gandhi page — here is the sequence that changed Indian politics.

1915

Gandhi returns

He lands in Bombay on 9 January after 21 years in South Africa, bringing the method of satyagraha and a network of allies.

1917–18

Champaran, Ahmedabad, Kheda

Local campaigns among indigo farmers, mill workers and peasants establish Gandhi's reputation and his method of inquiry, negotiation and non-violent pressure.

1919

Rowlatt Satyagraha and Jallianwala Bagh

Protests against detention without trial end in the Amritsar massacre, in which hundreds are killed. Gandhi returns the medals he received from the British and turns to full-time politics.

1920–22

Non-Cooperation

Alliance with the Khilafat movement, boycott of British institutions, and mass recruitment turn Congress into a popular force. Gandhi calls it off after the Chauri Chaura violence and is jailed.

1930–34

Salt and civil disobedience

The Dandi March launches a nationwide defiance of the salt law. Gandhi attends the Round Table Conference, is jailed again, and negotiates the Poona Pact on Dalit representation.

1942

Quit India

With the war at India's doorstep, Congress demands immediate independence. The leadership is imprisoned, but the movement erupts across the country.

The other tradition

Revolutionaries and the INA

Not everyone believed in non-violence. Armed revolutionaries and the Indian National Army offered a different path — and a different kind of sacrifice.

Bhagat Singh and the HSRA

The Hindustan Socialist Republican Association combined revolutionary action with socialist ideas. Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were hanged in Lahore on 23 March 1931, aged 23, 24 and 23. Their trial speeches made them national figures.

The Kakori action

In 1925, Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan and colleagues stopped a train at Kakori to fund the revolutionary movement. Their trial and executions became a founding legend of the armed struggle.

Chandra Shekhar Azad

The HSRA's daring organiser evaded capture for years, famously vowing never to be taken alive. He died in a shootout at Alfred Park, Allahabad, in 1931.

Subhas Chandra Bose and the INA

Bose left the Congress, sought Axis help and built the Indian National Army from Indian prisoners of war in Southeast Asia. The INA's trials after the war generated enormous public sympathy and helped force the pace of independence.

1939–1947

The endgame

The Second World War broke the Raj's authority. The Viceroy declared war on India's behalf without consulting Indian leaders; Congress ministries resigned. The Cripps Mission of 1942 failed, and the Quit India movement that followed was crushed with mass arrests — but it made clear that the Raj could not continue indefinitely.

After the war, the INA trials, the mutinies of 1946 and the election of a Labour government in Britain changed the calculation. The Cabinet Mission of 1946 tried and failed to preserve a united India. Direct Action Day in August 1946 unleashed communal violence on a horrific scale. The Mountbatten Plan of June 1947 accepted partition, and the Indian Independence Act created two independent dominions. At midnight on 14–15 August 1947, India became free — and was divided.
