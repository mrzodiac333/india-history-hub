Freedom and the line

1947: Independence and Partition

At midnight on 14–15 August 1947, two new dominions were born. One of the largest migrations in human history began at the same moment, along a border drawn in five weeks by a man who had never set foot in the border districts.

1940–1947

How the division became inevitable

Demands for a separate Muslim homeland had been voiced since the early twentieth century, but they acquired mass force only in the 1940s. In March 1940, the Muslim League's Lahore Resolution called for autonomous states in Muslim-majority areas. The Congress, committed to a united secular India, rejected the idea. The war years deepened the impasse: the Quit India movement, the Bengal famine and the rapid growth of the League's organisation polarised politics.

After the war, the Cabinet Mission of 1946 proposed a loose federation with grouped provinces. The plan might have held the country together, but mutual distrust proved too great. The League called for Direct Action on 16 August 1946; the killings in Calcutta spread to Bihar, Noakhali and beyond. The Interim Government, formed in September, could not function as a coalition. By early 1947, communal violence and the collapse of authority convinced the British that partition was the only workable exit.

The League's position

The Muslim League argued that Muslims could not be secure in a Hindu-majority democracy and demanded a separate state in the Muslim-majority northwest and east.

The Congress position

Congress leaders insisted on a united, secular India with safeguards for minorities, and accepted partition only when it became clear that no alternative could command agreement.

June–August 1947

The Mountbatten Plan and the Radcliffe Line

On 3 June 1947, Viceroy Lord Mountbatten announced that Britain would transfer power on the basis of partition. Both Congress and the Muslim League accepted the plan, and the British Parliament passed the Indian Independence Act on 18 July. The princely states would be free to join either dominion or remain independent.

A Boundary Commission under the British lawyer Sir Cyril Radcliffe was given five weeks to draw the borders of Punjab and Bengal. Radcliffe arrived in India in July and had never visited the country before. He was given outdated maps, census data and competing claims, and he never visited the border districts. The award was published on 17 August, two days after independence, so that the celebrations would not be overshadowed by the line.

Dominion of India

A secular constitutional democracy, later a republic, inheriting the central government, most of the civil service and the bulk of the industrial economy. Jawaharlal Nehru became Prime Minister.

Dominion of Pakistan

Two wings separated by more than 1,600 kilometres of Indian territory: West Punjab, Sindh, the North-West Frontier and East Bengal. Muhammad Ali Jinnah became Governor-General.

Did you know?

The Radcliffe Line runs for roughly 2,900 kilometres through Punjab, Bengal and Assam. Radcliffe was paid a fee of £3,000 and later destroyed his papers. He never returned to India.

The human cost

Twelve to fifteen million people

The numbers are staggering and still debated. Between twelve and fifteen million people are estimated to have crossed the new borders in the largest migration in recorded history. Estimates of the dead range from several hundred thousand to two million. Entire towns changed population overnight; trains arrived filled with the dead; women were abducted, raped and murdered on a vast scale; families were separated and property abandoned.

The violence was not confined to one community or one region. Punjab, Bengal and the North-West Frontier Province saw the worst of it. Refugee camps in Delhi, Lahore, Calcutta and Karachi held hundreds of thousands. The new governments created ministries of rehabilitation, built new townships, and attempted to restore property and reunite families — tasks that continued for decades. Oral history projects, such as the Partition Museum in Amritsar and the 1947 Partition Archive, have preserved thousands of survivor testimonies.

The great integration

Princely states and the making of the Indian Union

At independence, more than 560 princely states covered roughly two-fifths of the subcontinent. The British had left them free to choose. Most, often after negotiation and pressure, acceded to India. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, as Deputy Prime Minister and Home Minister, and V. P. Menon, the civil servant who drafted the instruments of accession, led the process with a mixture of persuasion, guarantees and, where necessary, force.

Junagadh

The Nawab acceded to Pakistan despite a Hindu-majority population. India occupied the state in 1947 and a plebiscite in 1948 confirmed accession to India.

Hyderabad

The Nizam sought independence. After months of standoff and armed resistance, Operation Polo in September 1948 integrated the state into India.

Kashmir

After tribal fighters backed by Pakistan invaded in October 1947, Maharaja Hari Singh signed the Instrument of Accession. The first India–Pakistan war ended with a UN ceasefire and the Line of Control in 1949.

Integration was not uniform. Some states joined willingly on generous terms; others were pressured; a few resisted. The result was a union that, for all its faults, held together where many observers had predicted fragmentation.

Aftermath

A birth shadowed by grief

Independence was celebrated in Delhi with Nehru's “tryst with destiny” speech, even as Punjab burned. Gandhi, who had opposed partition and walked through riot-torn villages in Bengal and Bihar calling for peace, was assassinated in Delhi on 30 January 1948. The new state had to build a government, absorb refugees, integrate princely states, fight a war in Kashmir and write a constitution — all at once.

Pakistan's own birth was equally traumatic: the new state had no administrative capital ready, faced the same refugee crisis in reverse, and lost Jinnah in September 1948. The two nations would fight further wars in 1965 and 1971, and their relationship remains one of the most consequential and contested inheritances of 1947.

Remembering Partition

For decades, Partition was a silence in Indian public memory. Since the 1990s, historians, writers and filmmakers have documented survivor testimonies and family histories. The Partition Museum in Amritsar, opened in 2016, is the first museum dedicated to the subject. The 1947 Partition Archive collects oral histories from survivors around the world.
