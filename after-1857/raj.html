Crown rule

The British Raj, 1858–1914

After 1857, India was governed from London through a Viceroy and an expanding bureaucracy. Railways and telegraphs bound the subcontinent together — and so did famine, taxation and the first stirrings of a national movement.

Government Economy Society Princely states Nationalism
The machinery of rule

How the Raj worked

After 1858 the Secretary of State for India sat in the British cabinet, answerable to Parliament, and a Viceroy represented the Crown in India. Beneath them stood a vast administrative machine: the Indian Civil Service, the provincial governments, district officers, police, courts and the army. Indians were employed in large numbers but excluded from the highest ranks; the ICS examinations were held in London until 1922, and entry was effectively restricted by age and travel costs.

Representation was tightly controlled. The Indian Councils Acts of 1861 and 1892 added nominated and indirectly elected Indians to legislative councils, but real power remained with the executive. Local self-government, promoted by Ripon in the 1880s, created municipalities and district boards — schools of politics for a new Indian middle class.

The colonial economy

Railways, famine and the drain of wealth

British investment transformed India's physical landscape. The first passenger railway ran from Bombay to Thane in 1853; by 1900 the network exceeded 40,000 kilometres, carrying troops, grain and cotton to ports. Telegraph lines, canals and ports followed. These were real achievements — but they were built to serve imperial trade and administration, and their costs were borne by Indian taxpayers.

Deindustrialisation

Machine-made cloth from Lancashire undercut Indian handloom production. Artisans lost livelihoods, and India exported raw cotton and imported finished goods — a classic colonial division of labour.

The drain of wealth

Dadabhai Naoroji argued that pensions, home charges, military costs and remittances transferred India's wealth to Britain without return. His “drain theory” became a founding argument of Indian economic nationalism.

The revenue system

The Permanent Settlement in Bengal and the ryotwari and mahalwari systems elsewhere fixed or assessed land revenue rigidly. Cash payment and fixed demand made peasants vulnerable to drought and moneylenders.

Famines

Famines of 1876–78, 1896–97 and 1899–1900 killed millions. Relief was governed by laissez-faire doctrine; the famine commissions that followed slowly changed policy, but vulnerability remained.

Did you know?

The first train in India ran 34 kilometres from Bombay to Thane on 16 April 1853. The first telegraph line, from Calcutta to Agra, opened in 1854. By the end of the Raj, India had one of Asia's largest railway networks — a system built largely with Indian capital and labour, managed for imperial purposes.

Society and reform

Education, law and the making of a public sphere

Macaulay's Minute on Education (1835) had already pushed the Company towards English education. After 1857, universities were founded in Calcutta, Bombay and Madras (1857), and a Western-educated professional class — lawyers, teachers, journalists, doctors — grew in the cities. The census, begun in 1871, classified Indians by caste, religion and occupation, fixing categories that would shape politics for generations.

Social legislation continued: the Age of Consent Act (1891) raised the age of consent, and reformers campaigned against child marriage and caste discrimination. But reform was contested. The Raj's professed religious neutrality coexisted with social conservatism, and many nationalists argued that social reform must come from Indian society itself, not from a foreign government.

Print and public opinion

Newspapers in English and Indian languages created a national conversation. The Vernacular Press Act of 1878 tried to control it; its repeal in 1882 showed the power of organised protest.

Women and reform

Campaigns around widow remarriage, age of consent and women's education gathered pace, led by reformers such as Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar, Jyotirao Phule and Pandita Ramabai.

Religious revival

The Brahmo Samaj, Arya Samaj, Ramakrishna Mission and Aligarh movement reshaped religious and educational life. Swami Vivekananda's 1893 Chicago address carried Indian thought worldwide.

The map of the Raj

Princely states and the logic of paramountcy

Roughly two-fifths of the subcontinent's territory and a quarter of its population lived under princely rulers — some 565 states at independence. After 1858 the Crown promised not to annex them further; in return, the states accepted British paramountcy over defence and external affairs. Residents at the courts advised, and often controlled, the rulers.

The states were extraordinarily varied: Hyderabad and Mysore were large and relatively modern; many others were tiny. Some rulers built railways, schools and universities; others presided over severe inequality and limited civil rights. The political question of the states would return with force in 1947, when integration became the new nation's first great administrative challenge.

The stirrings

The birth of organised nationalism

The Indian National Congress was founded in Bombay in December 1885 by seventy-two delegates, with the encouragement of retired British officials. Its early leaders — Dadabhai Naoroji, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta, W. C. Bonnerjee — believed in constitutional agitation, reasoned argument and gradual reform. They petitioned, wrote and debated; they did not, at first, demand independence.

By the turn of the century a younger, more impatient generation had emerged. The partition of Bengal in 1905 — Curzon's attempt to split a politically active province — provoked the Swadeshi movement: boycott of British goods, promotion of Indian industry, and mass mobilisation. The Muslim League was founded in 1906, and the Morley–Minto reforms of 1909 introduced separate electorates. The Raj had entered its most contested decade.

Why 1885 matters

The Congress gave Indian nationalism a permanent organisation, an all-India platform and a vocabulary of rights and representation. Within twenty years it would move from petitioning to mass politics — and by 1947 it would negotiate the transfer of power.
