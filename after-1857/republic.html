The Republic

The Republic, 1950 to today

A constitution written in less than three years, a democracy built on universal adult franchise, and a state that has survived wars, famine, emergency and extraordinary change. This is the story of independent India.

1946–1950

The Constitution and the republic

The Constituent Assembly first met on 9 December 1946. Over two years, eleven months and eighteen days, its members debated the shape of the new state: federalism, fundamental rights, secularism, affirmative action, the powers of the President and the independence of the judiciary. B. R. Ambedkar, chair of the Drafting Committee, was the central figure in the final text. The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950 — the date chosen to honour the Congress's 1930 declaration of Purna Swaraj.

India became a sovereign democratic republic, with a parliamentary system, an independent Supreme Court, and a commitment to liberty, equality and fraternity. Rajendra Prasad became the first President; Jawaharlal Nehru continued as Prime Minister. The first general election, held over 1951–52, was the largest exercise of universal adult franchise in history at the time — and it proved that democracy could work in a poor, newly independent country.

1950–1964

The Nehru era

Nehru's India was optimistic and state-led. The Five-Year Plans, drafted by the Planning Commission, directed investment into agriculture, irrigation, steel and heavy industry. The Bhakra Nangal and Hirakud dams, the steel plants at Bhilai and Rourkela, and the Indian Institutes of Technology were symbols of a modernising nation. The States Reorganisation Act of 1956 redrew the internal map along linguistic lines, accommodating regional identity within the union.

Abroad, Nehru championed non-alignment, co-founded the Non-Aligned Movement in 1961, and advocated peaceful coexistence. The dream was shaken by the Sino-Indian War of 1962, a defeat that exposed India's military unpreparedness and changed its defence policy for decades. Nehru died in May 1964, having dominated Indian politics since independence.

1964–1991

Shastri, Indira and the Emergency

Lal Bahadur Shastri guided India through the 1965 war and signed the Tashkent Declaration, dying there in January 1966. Indira Gandhi then began a period of centralised, often populist rule. She nationalised fourteen major banks in 1969, presided over the Green Revolution's spread, and led India to a decisive victory in the 1971 war that created Bangladesh. In 1974, India conducted its first nuclear test.

In June 1975, facing a court verdict against her election, Indira Gandhi declared a national emergency. Civil liberties were suspended, opposition leaders were jailed and the press was censored. The Emergency lasted twenty-one months; when elections were held in 1977, she lost, and the Janata Party formed India's first non-Congress government. The episode remains a defining test of Indian democracy: institutions were damaged, but elections restored civilian rule.

Indira returned to power in 1980. Her second term was marked by regional conflicts, including the Punjab insurgency. In June 1984, Operation Blue Star at the Golden Temple was followed by her assassination on 31 October. Rajiv Gandhi became Prime Minister amid grief and hope; the Bhopal gas disaster of December 1984, one of the world's worst industrial accidents, shadowed the transition.

1991

The crisis that opened the economy

By 1991 India faced a balance-of-payments crisis: foreign reserves were nearly exhausted, inflation was high, and the Gulf War had pushed oil prices up. The government of P. V. Narasimha Rao, with Manmohan Singh as Finance Minister, responded by devaluing the rupee, dismantling industrial licensing, lowering tariffs and opening the economy to foreign investment. The “licence raj” that had shaped Indian business since the 1950s began to recede.

The reforms were politically contested and socially uneven, but they transformed the trajectory of the economy. Growth accelerated, a services and IT sector emerged, and a new middle class expanded. The same decade brought political turbulence: the Mandal Commission reservations, the demolition of the Babri Masjid in 1992, and the Bombay bombings of 1993. India's democracy was becoming more representative and more disputatious at the same time.

1998–today

Modern India

The late 1990s saw the rise of coalition politics and a new assertiveness abroad. The Vajpayee government conducted the Pokhran-II nuclear tests in 1998 and fought the Kargil War in 1999. The Congress-led UPA government of Manmohan Singh (2004–14) delivered high growth, the Right to Information Act and the India–US nuclear deal, while facing the Mumbai attacks of 26 November 2008.

In 2014, Narendra Modi led the BJP to a single-party majority, a first since 1984. His government combined economic reform — GST, demonetisation, digital public infrastructure — with a muscular foreign policy and a Hindu-nationalist cultural agenda. Article 370 was revoked in 2019, the COVID-19 pandemic tested the health system in 2020–21, and India landed Chandrayaan-3 near the Moon's south pole in 2023 while hosting the G20. In 2024, Modi was sworn in for a third term at the head of a coalition.

Today India is the world's most populous country, a nuclear power, a spacefaring nation and one of the largest economies. It remains, as it has been since 1950, a democracy of extraordinary diversity — and a work in progress.
