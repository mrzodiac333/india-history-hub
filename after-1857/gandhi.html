The Mahatma

Gandhi: from South Africa to the nation

He arrived in Bombay on 9 January 1915 with a method, a moral vocabulary and no official position. Within a decade, Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi had turned the Indian freedom struggle into the largest non-violent movement the world had seen.

1869–1914

A lawyer in search of a method

Gandhi was born on 2 October 1869 in Porbandar, a small princely state in Kathiawar, into a family of administrators. He studied law in London, returned to India as a shy and unsuccessful barrister, and in 1893 accepted a one-year case in South Africa. What was meant to be a brief stay became twenty-one years.

In South Africa he encountered racial discrimination directly — famously being thrown off a train at Pietermaritzburg for travelling in a first-class compartment. He stayed to organise the Indian community, founded the Natal Indian Congress, and developed satyagraha, the method of non-violent resistance based on truth and the refusal to cooperate with injustice. He also founded the Phoenix Settlement and Tolstoy Farm, communities built around simple living and self-reliance.

The laboratory

South Africa, 1893–1914

1893

Arrival in Durban

Gandhi arrives to represent an Indian merchant. A week later, he is thrown off a train at Pietermaritzburg — the experience that sets him on a new path.

1894

Natal Indian Congress

He helps found the organisation to resist discriminatory laws against Indians, and begins petitioning, writing and organising.

1906

First satyagraha

Against the Transvaal's registration laws, Gandhi launches his first mass campaign of non-violent resistance. He is jailed several times.

1910

Tolstoy Farm

Gandhi establishes a commune near Johannesburg where Indians of different religions and castes live and work together. He corresponds with Tolstoy.

1913

The great march

Miners' strikes and a march of indentured labourers lead to mass arrests and, eventually, a negotiated settlement with General Smuts.

1914

Farewell

Gandhi leaves South Africa for London, then India. His South African method — disciplined, non-violent, morally framed — will now be tested on an imperial scale.

9 January 1915

The return to India

Gandhi landed in Bombay in January 1915 to a hero's welcome among Indian nationalists. He had already been in contact with Gopal Krishna Gokhale, who became his political mentor and advised him to spend a year travelling and listening before entering politics. Gandhi obeyed. He toured the country, observed its poverty and diversity, and established an ashram — first at Kochrab in Ahmedabad, then in 1917 at Sabarmati.

His first Indian campaigns were local and concrete. In Champaran (1917), he investigated the exploitation of indigo farmers by European planters and won relief through inquiry and negotiation. In Ahmedabad (1918), he supported striking mill workers. In Kheda (1918), he backed peasants seeking remission of revenue after crop failure. These campaigns were small in scale but decisive in method: investigate, document, negotiate, and mobilise disciplined non-violent pressure.

Why the return matters

Before 1915, Indian nationalism was largely a movement of lawyers and newspapers. After 1915, it increasingly became a movement of peasants, workers and students — organised by a leader who could speak to them in their own terms. The date of Gandhi's return, 9 January, is now observed as Pravasi Bharatiya Divas.

The campaigns

Turning points in Indian politics

1917

Champaran

Gandhi's inquiry into indigo cultivation forces the government to appoint a committee and grant relief to peasants. It is his first satyagraha in India.

1919

Rowlatt Satyagraha and Jallianwala Bagh

A nationwide protest against detention without trial is met with repression. The Amritsar massacre kills hundreds of unarmed people. Gandhi emerges as the leading critic of the Raj.

1920–22

Non-Cooperation

Allied with the Khilafat movement, Gandhi urges the boycott of British schools, courts, councils and goods. Mass support is unprecedented. After the Chauri Chaura violence, he withdraws the movement and is jailed.

1930

The Salt March

Gandhi walks 240 miles from Sabarmati to Dandi to make salt in defiance of the salt law. Civil disobedience spreads nationwide; tens of thousands are arrested. Time magazine names him Man of the Year.

1931–32

Round Table and the Poona Pact

Gandhi attends the Second Round Table Conference in London. Back in India, he begins a fast against separate electorates for Dalits, ending with the Poona Pact negotiated with B. R. Ambedkar.

1942

Quit India

Gandhi demands immediate independence and gives the call “Do or Die.” The leadership is imprisoned at the Aga Khan Palace; the movement continues without them.

1947–48

Partition and assassination

Gandhi walks through riot-torn Noakhali and Bihar, fasting for communal peace. He is assassinated in Delhi on 30 January 1948 by Nathuram Godse.

The ideas

What Gandhi believed

Satyagraha

“Truth-force”: resistance through truth and non-cooperation, appealing to the conscience of the opponent rather than seeking to destroy them.

Ahimsa

Non-violence as a positive moral discipline, not merely the absence of violence. Means and ends must be consistent; an unjust method corrupts a just cause.

Swaraj

Self-rule as more than independence: self-government of the self, village republics, and freedom from both foreign rule and internal exploitation.

Swadeshi

Local production and self-reliance — the spinning wheel as a symbol of economic independence and dignity for the poor.

Trusteeship

Wealth should be held as a trust for society, not as private property to be used without responsibility. He pressed industrialists to act as trustees.

Communal harmony

Hindu-Muslim unity was central to his politics. His insistence on it cost him support and ultimately his life.

Legacy

A contested, indispensable figure

Gandhi's achievements are extraordinary: he made the Congress a mass organisation, brought peasants and workers into national politics, and demonstrated that disciplined non-violence could shake an empire. His campaigns created the moral and political pressure that made independence possible. He also had significant failures: he could not prevent Partition, his fasts and compromises on Dalit representation were fiercely criticised by Ambedkar, and his early South African politics did not challenge white supremacy as fully as later anti-colonial leaders would.

He was not a saint, and he did not claim to be. He was a political strategist of unusual moral intensity, who tested his ideas on himself and often changed his mind. His influence extends far beyond India — from Martin Luther King Jr. and the American civil rights movement to anti-colonial struggles across Africa and Asia. In India, his birthday, 2 October, is a national holiday, and he is remembered as the Father of the Nation.
