The big questions

Frequently asked

Was the Revolt of 1857 a mutiny or the First War of Independence?

Both descriptions capture part of the truth. It began as a mutiny in the Bengal Army and was long called the “Sepoy Mutiny” by the British. Indian nationalists, especially V. D. Savarkar in 1909, called it the First War of Independence. Most historians now describe a broad anti-colonial uprising with genuine political aims but limited coordination and many local grievances. Its consequences were certainly revolutionary: it ended Company rule and began the Crown Raj.

What changed after 1857?

The East India Company was abolished and India passed to the British Crown through the Government of India Act 1858. A Secretary of State in London and a Viceroy in India took charge. The army was reorganised to prevent another coordinated revolt, the Crown promised not to annex more princely states, and the Raj began to cultivate princes, landlords and religious communities as counterweights to nationalism.

How did the British Raj govern India?

Through a Viceroy answerable to a Secretary of State in London, an elite Indian Civil Service, provincial governors, district officers, police and courts. Legislative councils included nominated and later elected Indians, but real power stayed with the executive. Roughly two-fifths of the subcontinent remained under princely rulers who accepted British paramountcy.

What was the “drain of wealth”?

Dadabhai Naoroji and other nationalist economists argued that Britain extracted wealth from India through home charges, pensions, military costs, remittances and unfavourable trade, without returning equivalent value. This “drain theory” became a central argument of the freedom movement and a critique of colonial economic policy.

Why did famines recur under the Raj?

Colonial revenue systems, the shift to cash crops, moneylender debt and fragile rural incomes made peasants vulnerable to drought. Relief policy, influenced by laissez-faire ideas, was often inadequate. Major famines struck in 1876–78, 1896–97, 1899–1900 and 1943. Millions died, and the famines became a powerful indictment of colonial rule.

What was dyarchy?

A system introduced by the Government of India Act 1919 under which provincial subjects were divided into “transferred” subjects administered by elected Indian ministers and “reserved” subjects controlled by the governor. It was meant to give Indians experience of government, but the division of power made it unworkable, and it was abolished by the Act of 1935.

What was the Rowlatt Act?

The Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act of 1919 allowed the government to detain suspects without trial for up to two years. Passed despite unanimous Indian opposition in the Imperial Legislative Council, it triggered Gandhi's first nationwide satyagraha and set the stage for the Jallianwala Bagh massacre.

What happened at Jallianwala Bagh?

On 13 April 1919, General Reginald Dyer ordered troops to fire on an unarmed gathering at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, killing hundreds and wounding more than a thousand. The Hunter Commission criticised Dyer, but the broader response was lenient, and the massacre became a turning point in Indian attitudes to British rule.

Why did Gandhi launch the Non-Cooperation Movement?

Gandhi combined outrage at the Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh with the Khilafat grievance and the demand for swaraj. Launched in 1920, the movement urged Indians to boycott British schools, courts, titles and goods. It was withdrawn in 1922 after the Chauri Chaura violence, because Gandhi insisted that non-violence was non-negotiable.

What was the Dandi March?

In March–April 1930, Gandhi walked about 240 miles from Sabarmati Ashram to the coastal village of Dandi to make salt in defiance of the British salt monopoly. The march launched the Civil Disobedience Movement, which spread across India and led to tens of thousands of arrests.

What was the Quit India Movement?

Launched on 8 August 1942, it demanded an immediate end to British rule. Gandhi's slogan was “Do or Die.” The Congress leadership was arrested, but the movement became a widespread, largely leaderless rebellion. It convinced many British officials that the Raj could not continue after the war.

Why was India partitioned?

There is no single cause. The Muslim League's demand for Pakistan, the Congress's commitment to a united India, the polarisation of the 1940s, the Second World War, the failure of the Cabinet Mission, and escalating communal violence all contributed. Britain, anxious to leave, accepted partition as the only workable transfer of power. The decision was made quickly, and the human cost was catastrophic.

What was the Radcliffe Line?

The boundary between India and Pakistan in Punjab and Bengal, drawn by a commission chaired by Sir Cyril Radcliffe in 1947. Radcliffe had never visited India before and was given about five weeks to decide. The award was published on 17 August 1947, two days after independence.

How were the princely states integrated into India?

At independence there were more than 560 princely states. Most acceded to India through negotiation, guarantees of autonomy and pressure, led by Sardar Patel and V. P. Menon. Junagadh was resolved by occupation and a plebiscite; Hyderabad was integrated by force in 1948; Kashmir's accession was followed by war and remains disputed. Goa, a Portuguese colony rather than a princely state, was integrated in 1961.

When did India become a republic?

The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950, when India became a sovereign democratic republic. Independence Day is 15 August 1947; Republic Day is 26 January 1950.

What was the Emergency?

On 25 June 1975, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi declared a national emergency after a court set aside her election. Civil liberties were suspended, opposition leaders were jailed and the press was censored. Elections held in 1977 brought the Janata Party to power and ended the Emergency — a defining test of Indian democracy.

What was the 1991 economic liberalisation?

Facing a balance-of-payments crisis, the government of P. V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Manmohan Singh devalued the rupee, removed industrial licensing, lowered tariffs and opened India to foreign investment. The reforms ended the “licence raj” and set India on a higher-growth path.

Is Hindi the national language of India?

India has no national language. Hindi and English are the official languages of the Union, and the Constitution recognises 22 scheduled languages. States have their own official languages, and the Eighth Schedule has been expanded since 1950.

Why is Gandhi called the Father of the Nation?

The title is a popular honorific rather than a constitutional one. It reflects his central role in the mass freedom movement and his moral authority across communities. It is often attributed to Subhas Chandra Bose, who reportedly addressed him as the father of the nation in a 1944 radio broadcast.

Terms

Glossary

Swaraj
Self-rule; in Gandhi's usage, self-government of the individual and the community as well as political independence.
Satyagraha
“Truth-force”: non-violent resistance through truth, discipline and non-cooperation.
Ahimsa
Non-violence; a positive moral discipline central to Gandhi's thought.
Swadeshi
Economic self-reliance; the use and promotion of Indian-made goods.
Purna Swaraj
Complete independence, adopted by the Congress as its goal in 1929.
Dyarchy
Dual government in the provinces under the 1919 Act, dividing transferred and reserved subjects.
Home Rule
Self-government within the Empire, demanded by the Home Rule Leagues from 1916.
Khilafat
The movement to protect the Ottoman Caliphate, allied with Gandhi's Non-Cooperation campaign.
Rowlatt Act
The 1919 law allowing detention without trial; the target of Gandhi's first nationwide satyagraha.
Quit India
The 1942 movement demanding immediate British withdrawal from India.
Radcliffe Line
The 1947 boundary between India and Pakistan in Punjab and Bengal.
Instrument of Accession
The legal document by which princely states joined India or Pakistan in 1947.
Dominion
A self-governing state within the British Commonwealth; India and Pakistan were dominions from 1947.
Republic
A state whose head is elected, not a monarch; India became a republic on 26 January 1950.
Secularism
In the Indian Constitution, equal respect for all religions rather than a strict separation of religion and state.
Non-alignment
The Cold War policy of not joining either superpower bloc, championed by Nehru.
Licence Raj
The pre-1991 system of industrial licensing and state controls that shaped the Indian economy.
Green Revolution
The 1960s–70s adoption of high-yielding seeds, fertiliser and irrigation that transformed Indian agriculture.
Scheduled Castes and Tribes
Communities formally recognised by the Constitution for protective and affirmative-action measures.
